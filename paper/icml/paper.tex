\begin{abstract}
Reinforcement learning with verifiable rewards (RLVR) methods often report pass@$k$ at equal training steps, although their pass@1 can differ. We compare methods at matched pass@1 using pre-registered criteria and bootstrap intervals over problems. Under independent on-policy sampling, correctness-only advantage rules have expected per-problem updates proportional to the gradient of success probability; we derive their exact second moments, allowing reward-dependent stopping. At initialization, incorrect-to-correct squared score-norm ratios are roughly 2.1--3.4. Negative-sample and positive-sample reinforcement satisfy average matched-accuracy pass@16 equivalence within $\pm2$ points on Qwen2.5-Math-1.5B and OLMo-2-0425-1B-SFT. On Qwen, trace-matched zero-mean sign noise added to positive reinforcement reproduces the pooled early accuracy lag of negative reinforcement, with seed heterogeneity. Coverage-weighted replay gives a small positive mean gain of $+1.19$ points, 95\% $[+0.54,+1.79]$, below the registered frontier-improvement threshold. A per-problem log-odds model, initialized from base success counts and conditioned on checkpoint pass@1, has test-set pass@16 RMSE of 1.21 points for nine further Qwen rules and 1.90 points on OLMo. The frozen model fails its registered criterion after a supervised warm start.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
RL with verifiable rewards trains a language model on automatically checked answers. For binary rewards, many methods modify the advantage in $g=\sum_i A_i\nabla\log\pi(y_i)$, changing the weights assigned to correct and incorrect samples. Positive- and negative-sample reinforcement are a useful test case: they use only correct or only incorrect samples, respectively, yet have equal expected policy-gradient updates under on-policy sampling \citep{zhu2025nsr,williams1992,greensmith2004}. Normalization changes \citep{liu2025drgrpo} and pass@$k$ objectives \citep{walder2025pkpo,thrampoulidis2025shaping} provide further comparisons. We study whether these choices change the accuracy--diversity trade-off measured by pass@1 and pass@$k$.

Fixed-step comparisons mix progress in pass@1 with changes in pass@$k$. A higher pass@$k$ can reflect a different position on a learning trajectory, so it does not by itself establish a better accuracy--diversity trade-off. We ask how much pass@$k$ each method retains at matched pass@1. Matched-accuracy plots already appear in DARS, which reports higher pass@128 at fixed pass@1 \citep{yang2025depth}. We add pre-registered decision criteria and problem-level bootstrap intervals that recompute the interpolated reference. Observed comparisons use only checkpoints within the reference's pass@1 support.

We separate the effect of advantage design on the gradient estimator from its effect on the accuracy--diversity trade-off. With independent on-policy sampling and bounded reward-dependent stopping, a correctness-only rule has expected per-problem update $c(p_x)\nabla p_x$. We derive its exact second moment, allowing unequal conditional score norms for correct and incorrect answers. The mean identity agrees with objective-based analyses \citep{davis2025objective}; the second moment guides our noise-injection experiment. NSR and PSR satisfy pre-registered average matched-accuracy pass@16 equivalence on Qwen2.5-Math-1.5B and OLMo-2-0425-1B-SFT. On Qwen, trace-matched zero-mean sign noise added to PSR reproduces NSR's pooled early accuracy lag, with seed heterogeneity. The registered OLMo speed prediction fails, and its pass@4 comparison remains inconclusive.

At matched pass@1, pass@$k$ is a concave average of per-problem success probabilities. For Dr.\ GRPO, a group of eight rollouts with no correct answer has zero advantages, so multiplicative reweighting cannot make that group contribute an update. This motivates replay and larger groups as interventions on the availability of successful samples. Coverage-weighted replay gives a mean gain of $+1.19$ pass@16 points, below the registered $+2$ threshold. Groups of 32 and a supervised warm start reach higher accuracy after 150 steps, but their mean endpoints lie beyond the available reference range; the longer-reference comparisons remain interim.

We also test a descriptive model of per-problem log-odds learning, with one transfer coefficient fitted on Dr.\ GRPO. Using base-model success counts and each checkpoint's observed pass@1, it predicts aggregate pass@16 with test-set RMSE of 1.21 points for nine further Qwen rules and 1.90 points on OLMo. It fails after the supervised warm start, and its longer-training test remains incomplete.

The core registered comparisons and their outcomes are summarized in \cref{app:prereg}. Interim estimates and exploratory diagnostics are identified in the text.

\section{Setup and the matched-accuracy protocol}\label{sec:setup}
For a problem $x$ with reward $r\in\{0,1\}$, the policy's success probability is $p_x=\Pr_\pi(r=1\mid x)$, the score of an answer is $\ell(y)=\nabla_\vartheta\log\pi(y\mid x)$, and $F_r=\E[\|\ell\|^2\mid r]$ is the mean squared score norm of correct ($r=1$) and incorrect ($r=0$) answers. Unless stated otherwise, a training step samples $G=8$ rollouts for each of 16 problems. Dr.\ GRPO uses $A_i=r_i-\bar r$ within a group \citep{liu2025drgrpo}; positive-sample reinforcement (PSR) uses $A_i=r_i$ and negative-sample reinforcement (NSR) $A_i=r_i-1$ \citep{zhu2025nsr}. Over $N$ problems,
\begin{align*}P_k&=\tfrac1N\textstyle\sum_x\big[1-(1-p_x)^k\big],\\ \widehat P_k&=\tfrac1N\textstyle\sum_x\Big[1-\binom{n_x-C_x}{k}\Big/\binom{n_x}{k}\Big],\end{align*}
where $C_x$ counts correct answers among $n_x$ independent samples and $\widehat P_k$ is unbiased for $1\le k\le n_x$ \citep{chen2021codex}. The main Qwen replay evaluations use $n_x=32$.

\begin{proposition}[pass@$k$ at matched pass@1 measures spread]\label{prop:spread}
$P_1=\E_x p_x$ and $P_k=\E_x f_k(p_x)$ with $f_k(p)=1-(1-p)^k$ strictly concave for $k>1$. Among distributions of $\{p_x\}$ with the same mean, a mean-preserving spread lowers $P_k$; $P_k$ is largest when all $p_x$ are equal.
\end{proposition}

\textbf{Protocol.} We identify the reference for each comparison. For the primary coverage-weighted and base-seeded replay contrasts, $E_B(a)=\max\{L_{\rm steps}(a),L_T(a)\}$ is the larger of the linear interpolations in pass@1 along Dr.\ GRPO's trajectory (base model and steps 50, 100, 150) and its step-150 temperature sweep ($T\in\{1,1.15,1.3\}$), each within its supported range. NSR contrasts use PSR as the reference; uniform-replay and unlikeliness audits use Dr.\ GRPO's trajectory alone. Interim longer-training contrasts use the available step-250 trajectory. Gains are averaged over qualifying checkpoints within each run, then over seeds. Intervals use 2,000 bootstrap draws over problems, with the reference recomputed in every draw, conditional on the observed seeds. Observed checkpoints are restricted to the reference's pass@1 range; bootstrap endpoint clamping is disclosed in \cref{app:prereg}. We call a difference \emph{average equivalence} if its 90\% interval lies within $\pm2$ points and say a method \emph{moves the frontier} if its gain is at least $+2$ points with the 95\% interval above zero in every seed.

\section{Theory}\label{sec:theory}
\begin{theorem}[Two moments of correctness-only rules]\label{thm:moments}
Let a rule draw answers i.i.d.\ from $\pi(\cdot\mid x)$, stop at a time $N\le n_{\max}$ that depends only on the rewards observed so far, and assign advantages that depend only on the rewards. Then $g=\sum_{i\le N}A_i\ell(y_i)$ satisfies
\begin{align*}\E g&=c(p)\nabla p,\\ \E\|g\|^2&=F_1D_1(p)+F_0D_0(p)+\|\nabla p\|^2X(p),\end{align*}
where $c$, $D_1$, $D_0$, $X$ and $\E N$ depend only on the rule and on $p$.
\end{theorem}
The proof conditions on the reward sequence, given which $N$ and the advantages are fixed and $\E[\ell\mid r=1]=\nabla p/p$, $\E[\ell\mid r=0]=-\nabla p/(1-p)$ (\cref{app:proofs}). For fixed groups the mean part is known \citep{davis2025objective}, and the equality of the PSR and NSR means is classical baseline invariance \citep{williams1992,greensmith2004}. The second-moment part, with $F_0\neq F_1$ and reward-dependent stopping, is what lets us predict and manipulate noise.

\begin{corollary}\label{cor:psrnsr}
With advantage $a$ for correct and $b$ for incorrect answers in a group of $n$, $\E g=n(a-b)\nabla p$. PSR and NSR have the same mean $n\nabla p$, and NSR equals PSR plus the zero-mean term $-\sum_i\ell(y_i)$; when $F_0>F_1$, NSR has the larger second moment on every problem with $p<F_0/(F_0+F_1)$.
\end{corollary}

\begin{lemma}[Rarely solved problems receive sparse updates]\label{lem:starve}
Under Dr.\ GRPO with a group of $n$, the update on problem $x$ is zero unless the group contains both a correct and an incorrect answer. This event has probability $1-p^n-(1-p)^n\approx np$ for small $p$. Multiplicative reweighting of Dr.\ GRPO's advantages cannot make an all-failure or all-success group contribute an update.
\end{lemma}

A common frontier does not follow from the moment theorem. The expected update on each problem is collinear with $\nabla p_x$, but changes in the per-problem coefficients can rotate the aggregate update through shared parameters \citep{barakat2026interference}. The sparse-update lemma establishes a property of Dr.\ GRPO and its multiplicative reweightings; it does not establish a necessary condition for every frontier improvement. It motivates testing replay and larger groups as ways to change which problems receive realized updates.

\section{Advantage rules at matched accuracy}\label{sec:audit}
\textbf{Models.} Qwen2.5-Math-1.5B \citep{yang2024qwen25math}: MATH training problems, 150 steps, answers up to 512 tokens, evaluation on 511 held-out MATH test problems. OLMo-2-0425-1B-SFT \citep{olmo2024}: 300 steps, 256 held-out problems, 1,024-token answers. Our default update uses an on-policy sequence-sum loss with AdamW at $10^{-6}$; the sample-reuse replication and replay interventions modify this setup. Label checks are described in \cref{app:labels}.

\textbf{Conditional score norms and update second moments.} At initialization, $F_0/F_1$ is 2.14 and 2.54 on OLMo and 2.7--3.4 on Qwen. On Qwen it falls to about 1.4--1.6 at steps 50--150, and the registered persistence criterion is inconclusive. In an exploratory diagnostic with $F_0$ and $F_1$ re-measured and interpolated during training, predicted and measured second moments agree within a factor of 0.69--1.18 in every 50-step window. Raw NSR/PSR second-moment ratios are about 6 on Qwen and 19--22 on OLMo. On Qwen, rescaling by Adam's previous per-coordinate scales reduces the ratio to 1.04--1.32.

\textbf{NSR and PSR agree at matched accuracy.} The two satisfy average matched-accuracy equivalence on both model families (\cref{tab:audit}). Their progress rates differ less consistently: the registered OLMo speed prediction fails because both methods reach the midpoint at 52 steps in seed 1; in seed 2, NSR takes 88 steps and PSR 41. An earlier Qwen on-policy experiment found slower NSR progress and equal final pass@64. A Qwen replication using the implementation of \citet{zhu2025nsr}, including sample reuse, clipping and token averaging, did not reproduce the reported advantage in this setting. Across constant baselines $A=r-b$, progress follows the ordering by predicted second moment (rank correlation 0.9--0.97), with matched-accuracy differences within about two points of PSR's trajectory.

\textbf{Noise injection reproduces the pooled early lag.} On Qwen, we add a zero-mean random-sign term to PSR, calibrated to match the same-policy NSR second moment. Over steps 25--49, the registered progress index is $\phi=-0.11$, with a 90\% interval $[-0.22,-0.05]$, where NSR is 0 and PSR is 1. PSR with noise is slightly slower than NSR in the pooled comparison, and seed heterogeneity is flagged. This perturbation reproduces the pooled early accuracy lag without identifying scalar noise magnitude as its unique mechanism. Doubling NSR's learning rate gives $\phi=1.16$ relative to PSR at its original rate; at the matched doubled rate, NSR still lags by 4.7 accuracy points, 95\% $[2.7,6.3]$, over two seeds. The Adam diagnostic measures the lagged, unnormalized projection onto a population-gradient reference: its NSR/PSR ratio is 0.73--0.87.

\textbf{Temperature control.} Higher-temperature evaluations of the late Qwen checkpoint have a mean matched-pass@1 difference of $-1.42$ pass@16 points, with a 90\% interval $[-2.19,-0.68]$, relative to the training trajectory. Entropy on fixed base-model text changes from 1.081 to 1.064, while entropy on policy-generated samples falls three- to fourfold. These diagnostics distinguish the tested temperature sweep from the training trajectory. Entropy on policy samples mixes changes in conditional token distributions with changes in the sampled contexts.

\textbf{Pass@$k$, unlikeliness reward, and entropy.} The pass@4 objective \citep{walder2025pkpo} gives matched-accuracy differences of $+1.6$ and $+0.2$ points on Qwen in two seeds. Its OLMo comparison is inconclusive under the registered rule (\cref{tab:audit}). Unlikeliness reward \citep{he2025unlikely} gives $+0.14$ points, 95\% $[-0.32,+0.62]$, over three seeds. Unlikeliness reward and entropy control depend on trajectory likelihood or token entropy and are outside the correctness-only theorem. In a one-seed pilot, entropy bonuses gave no gain, while entropy floors and adaptive control prevented accuracy progress.

\section{Replay, larger groups, and a supervised warm start}\label{sec:faster}
Dr.\ GRPO's zero advantages on all-failure groups motivate interventions that supply successful samples through replay or increased sampling. We evaluate replay, base-seeded replay, larger groups, and a supervised warm start on Qwen. The replay and warm-start arms use three seeds; the available larger-group results use two.

\textbf{Replay.} Coverage-weighted replay stores verified successes and adds a log-likelihood term, selecting problems in proportion to the posterior mean of $k\,p(1-p)^{k-1}=\partial f_k/\partial\log p$. Its mean gain is $+1.19$ points, 95\% $[+0.54,+1.79]$, against Dr.\ GRPO's trajectory-and-temperature envelope. Uniform replay gives $+0.61$, 95\% $[-0.04,+1.27]$, against the trajectory alone. The direct coverage-weighted versus uniform contrast is $+0.66$, below its registered $+1$ criterion. Coverage-weighted replay also lowers step-150 pass@1 by 2.05 points, despite its positive matched-accuracy gain.

The interim base-seeded replay analysis uses successes from 32 base-model samples per problem and gives $+1.02$ points, 95\% $[+0.23,+1.68]$, against the envelope, while lowering step-150 pass@1 by 5.35 points. The completed replay analyses meet the average-agreement criterion; the base-seeded estimate remains interim.

\textbf{More rollouts, and a warm start.} Dr.\ GRPO with groups of 32 (G32) and a supervised warm start on base-model successes (S32) reach mean pass@1 52.5 after 150 steps, compared with 44.1 for Dr.\ GRPO. These means use two available G32 seeds and three S32 seeds. An interim longer reference, Dr.\ GRPO seed 13, reaches (pass@1, pass@16) = (51.7, 78.6) at step 250. Averaged over checkpoints within its pass@1 range, the differences are $+0.60$ points for G32 (4 of 6 checkpoints) and $+2.38$ for S32 (10 of 12); the 150-step Dr.\ GRPO runs give $+0.47$. These are interim point estimates, and the reference does not cover the mean endpoints at pass@1 52.5. G32 also uses four times as many rollouts per step, while S32 includes an additional supervised stage.

\section{A per-problem model of the frontier}\label{sec:model}
We test a descriptive model of the frontier using empirical-Bayes estimates from 32 base-model samples to initialize each problem's log-odds. With $p_x=\sigma(\theta_x)$, the dynamics are
\[\dot\theta_x=p_x(1-p_x)+\tau\,\tfrac1N\textstyle\sum_{x'}p_{x'}(1-p_{x'}),\]
combining state-dependent own-problem growth with a uniform transfer term. We fitted $\tau=-0.20$ on Dr.\ GRPO's nine checkpoints and froze it before computing the registered test predictions. Each checkpoint's observed pass@1 determines its location on the model trajectory; pass@16 and stratum-level success are then predictions. The model therefore describes the frontier conditional on accuracy, rather than predicting training speed.

The frozen model has pass@16 RMSE of 1.21 points for nine further Qwen rules (56 available checkpoints), 1.90 points for five OLMo rules with $\tau$ transferred from Qwen (50 checkpoints), and 0.85 points for four 1,024-token evaluation checkpoints (\cref{fig:law}). The affine log-odds baseline has RMSE of 2.45, 12.58, and 1.27 points on these sets, respectively; the uniform-shift baseline ranges from 9.85 to 20.07 points. OLMo's aggregate RMSE coexists with larger mean stratum residuals: observed minus predicted pass@16 is $+6.0$ points in the zero-count stratum and $-6.8$ in the rare stratum. On Qwen, a uniform shift predicts pass@16 of 70--80 for problems with zero successes in 32 base samples, compared with an observed 24.0 for Dr.\ GRPO at step 150.

The model fails the registered warm-start test when initialized from the warm-start policy's own step-0 counts: its pass@16 RMSE is 3.56 points. The longer-training test remains incomplete, with the available Dr.\ GRPO reference measured through step 250 and the registered extrapolation verdict pending. Deep base sampling is also partial: three of four shards cover 232 of the 309 problems with at most four successes in the initial 32 samples. Using 256 additional samples per problem, the refitted coefficient is $\tau=+0.20$, with a 90\% interval $[+0.10,+0.35]$ from 200 bootstrap draws. Refining the initial success estimates changes the fitted coefficient from negative to positive; its interpretation as transfer therefore depends on the initialization and the assumed log-odds dynamics.

\section{Discussion}\label{sec:moves}
The mean-update identity leaves room for different frontier shapes through cross-problem weights and shared parameters. Our audits establish average matched-accuracy agreement for the tested NSR--PSR comparisons and a small positive replay gain. The longer-reference results are provisional point estimates from overlapping checkpoints. A model conditioned on observed pass@1 predicts aggregate pass@16 in several settings but fails after the supervised warm start, limiting its use as a description of training trajectories.

\section{Limitations}
Our experiments use 1B and 1.5B models, with main runs of 150 steps on Qwen and 300 on OLMo. Generalization to larger models and longer training horizons remains untested. The evaluation budget matters substantially: for Dr.\ GRPO seed 10 at step 150, increasing the budget from 512 to 1,024 tokens raises pass@1 from 44.6 to 57.7 and reduces truncation from 48\% to 15.5\%. The 1,024-token check remains partial. Bootstrap intervals cover problem sampling conditional on the observed training seeds; they do not estimate training-run variability. Average equivalence within $\pm2$ points permits smaller and checkpoint-specific differences. The longer-reference comparisons in \cref{sec:faster} are interim estimates against one seed and include only overlapping checkpoints.

\section{Proofs}\label{app:proofs}
\textbf{\Cref{thm:moments}.} Couple the rule to an i.i.d.\ sequence $y_1,y_2,\dots$ with rewards $r$. Given $r$, the stopping time $N$ and the advantages $A_i$ are fixed, the answers are independent, $\E[\ell(y_i)\mid r]=\kappa(r_i)\nabla p$ with $\kappa(1)=1/p$ and $\kappa(0)=-1/(1-p)$, and $\E[\|\ell(y_i)\|^2\mid r]=F_{r_i}$. Hence $\E[g\mid r]=\sum_{i\le N}A_i\kappa(r_i)\nabla p$ and $\E[\|g\|^2\mid r]=\sum_iA_i^2F_{r_i}+\sum_{i\neq j}A_iA_j\kappa(r_i)\kappa(r_j)\|\nabla p\|^2$; averaging over $r$, whose law depends only on $p$, gives the claim.
\textbf{\Cref{prop:spread}.} $f_k''(p)=-k(k-1)(1-p)^{k-2}<0$ for $k>1$; apply Jensen's inequality.
\textbf{\Cref{lem:starve}.} With all rewards equal, $r_i-\bar r=0$ for every $i$; a reweighting that depends only on the rewards multiplies these zeros.
