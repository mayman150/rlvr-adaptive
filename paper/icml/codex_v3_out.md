\section{Rollout allocation at a fixed budget}\label{sec:allocation}

Pre-registration~18 tests whether reallocating sampled answers changes pass@16 at matched pass@1 without increasing the rollout budget. Each arm uses $128$ rollouts per step on the same $16$ problems as Dr.\ GRPO, denoted $B$. Training runs for $150$ steps with $512$-token answers in seeds $8$, $9$, and $10$. Four pilot rollouts per problem determine the success count $c$; the remaining $64$ rollouts are distributed with a cap of $32$ per problem. Pilot and additional samples are pooled before computing Dr.\ GRPO advantages.

Frontier-value allocation (AC) assigns additional rollouts in proportion to the registered model value
\[
v(c)=\max\!\left\{0,\E\!\left[
p^2(1-p)^2\{16(1-p)^{15}-\lambda\}\mid c
\right]\right\},
\]
with $p\mid c\sim\operatorname{Beta}(c+1/2,4-c+1/2)$ and reference slope $\lambda=0.37$. Only $c=0$ has positive value under these settings; residual allocations after caps bind are distributed uniformly using largest-remainder rounding. Signal allocation (AV) instead weights additional rollouts by $\E[p(1-p)\mid c]$. Both retain the fixed normalizer. The HORA control, denoted HR, allocates greedily by posterior marginal hit gain under a $\operatorname{Beta}(1,1)$ prior and scales each problem's contribution by $8/N_x$, where $N_x$ is its final group size.

Rule~1 compares AC with the trajectory-and-temperature envelope $E_B$; rules~2 and~3 compare it with the AV and HR trajectories. Their thresholds are $+2$ points for rule~1 and $+1$ for rules~2 and~3, with positive 95\% intervals and Holm correction; rule~1 also requires positive gains in every seed. Gains are averaged over supported checkpoints and then equally over seeds; bootstrap intervals resample problems and condition on the trained seeds. Rules~1--3 are not met. AC gains $+0.14$ pass@16 points against $E_B$, 95\% $[-0.36,+0.58]$; its 90\% interval $[-0.28,+0.51]$ satisfies average agreement. AC versus AV gives $+0.51$, 95\% $[+0.04,+0.99]$, below its effect threshold and nonsignificant after Holm correction. AC versus HR gives $+0.00$, 95\% $[-0.52,+0.46]$. No confirmation run was triggered.

The descriptive mechanism diagnostics show that allocation operates as intended. Pilot-zero problems, defined by no success in the current pilot, receive a mean of $18.9$ rollouts under AC. The reported fraction of their final groups containing a success is $50$--$54\%$, compared with $18$--$21\%$ under AV. These diagnostics were reported without bootstrap intervals. Thus AC increases binary success signals on problems missed by the pilot without establishing a better matched-accuracy curve.

The registered point forecast check interpolates seed-mean curves at covered accuracy coordinates. Confirmation requires absolute error within $1.5$ points of the forecast mean over those coordinates. HR and AV forecasts are confirmed under both model variants. AC has observed mean gain $-0.05$ points against forecasts of $+4.12$ from the frozen law and $+2.46$ from deeper initial counts; neither forecast is confirmed. The per-problem frontier model therefore fails on the allocation intervention it was designed to predict. More realized successes on rarely solved problems do not, in this experiment, produce the predicted change in the curve.

\section{The answer budget as a confound}\label{sec:budget}

An answer budget $b$ caps the number of generated response tokens. For $M$ sampled answers, let $I_i(b)$ indicate whether answer $i$ is stopped by this cap. We define the \emph{truncation rate} as $\rho_b=M^{-1}\sum_{i=1}^{M}I_i(b)$. This rate matters even at matched pass@1: a length cap can affect different problems unequally, changing pass@16 at a fixed mean success probability. A comparison can therefore depend on response length as well as on the distribution of per-problem success probabilities.

At $512$ tokens, Dr.\ GRPO has a reported truncation rate of $48\%$, whereas the human-solution arms of Pre-registration~19 have rates of $6$--$7\%$. Both human-solution arms lie above its trajectory at this budget. Pre-registration~19 compares supervised training on human solutions for problems with observed base successes (HP) with a swap arm (HQ) that replaces some of those problems by problems with no observed base success. These strata refer to successes observed in $32$ base samples per problem.

Check~16 re-evaluates existing checkpoints at the larger answer budget, with no additional training. For the supervised warm start on base-model successes (S32), the pooled step-0 gain against the same-budget Dr.\ GRPO trajectory is $+4.77$ pass@16 points at $512$ tokens, 90\% $[+3.45,+6.07]$, and $+2.15$ at $1{,}024$ tokens, 90\% $[+1.10,+3.20]$. Under the reading fixed before re-evaluation, a gain below half its original magnitude is classified as \emph{largely a budget effect}. The completed check meets that condition while retaining a smaller positive gain. Later S32 checkpoints lie outside the available reference accuracy range at both budgets, so this comparison concerns the supervised initialization.

The registered Check~21 comparison uses checkpoints with common reference support at both budgets and paired bootstrap draws. The interim seed~10 observation reverses the ordering: the human-solution arm is below the same-budget Dr.\ GRPO trajectory at $1{,}024$ tokens. \pending{Check 21: remaining seeds and the registered pooled budget-effect verdict.} Separately, Pre-registration~19 rule~1 is not interpretable because HQ checkpoints in seeds~9 and~10 do not overlap the HP accuracy range. The descriptive step-150 zero-count stratum contrast, HQ minus HP, is $+0.58$ pass@16 points, 90\% $[-2.13,+3.43]$, and cannot substitute for the registered matched-accuracy test.

Matched pass@1 controls mean success, but does not control which answers are censored by a length limit. Claims about an accuracy--diversity frontier must state the answer budget, use supported comparisons within that budget, and assess sensitivity to differential truncation. Re-evaluation changes the measurement protocol; \pending{P21: replication with training and evaluation at 1,024 tokens.}

\begin{abstract}
Reinforcement learning with verifiable rewards (RLVR) methods are often compared at equal training steps despite differences in single-answer accuracy. We compare pass@$k$ at matched pass@1, defined by interpolating a reference to the same estimated single-answer accuracy. For correctness-only advantage rules under independent on-policy sampling, we derive expected per-problem updates and exact second moments with bounded reward-dependent stopping. Audits establish average matched-accuracy pass@16 equivalence between positive- and negative-sample reinforcement. At a fixed rollout budget, model-guided allocation supplies more successes to problems missed by a pilot, but meets none of the registered improvement rules: its pass@16 gain is $+0.14$ points, 95\% $[-0.36,+0.58]$. The model fails its allocation forecast. Increasing the evaluation budget from $512$ to $1{,}024$ tokens reduces the supervised warm-start step-0 gain from $+4.77$, 90\% $[+3.45,+6.07]$, to $+2.15$, 90\% $[+1.10,+3.20]$, meeting the registered criterion for largely a budget effect. An interim human-solution comparison reverses its ordering relative to the reference in seed~10. Matched-accuracy comparisons remain conditional on answer budget and reference support. \pending{Results of the 1,024-token training replication (P21), 7B replication (P20), and minimum-help pilot (P23).}
\end{abstract}

Fixed-step comparisons mix changes in single-answer accuracy with changes in repeated-sampling success. At \emph{matched pass@1}, we compare a method's measured $\widehat P_k$ with a reference $R(a)$ interpolated at the same estimated accuracy $a=\widehat P_1$; the gain is $\widehat P_k-R(a)$. For method $m$, a \emph{trajectory} is the ordered sequence of checkpoint measurements $\mathcal T_m=\{(\widehat P_1(\pi_{m,t}),\widehat P_k(\pi_{m,t}))\}_{t\in\mathcal K}$ at fixed decoding settings and answer budget. An \emph{envelope} is the pointwise maximum of supported reference curves. Here $E_B(a)=\max\{L_{\rm steps}(a),L_T(a)\}$ combines linear interpolation along Dr.\ GRPO's checkpoint trajectory with interpolation through a late-checkpoint temperature sweep; the maximum includes interpolants supported at $a$. Matched-accuracy comparisons have precedent in DARS \citep{yang2025depth}. Our protocol adds pre-registered decision criteria and problem-level bootstrap intervals that recompute the reference under resampling.

We derive the mean and exact second moment of correctness-only updates under independent on-policy sampling and bounded reward-dependent stopping, separating per-problem gradient scaling from estimator noise. We combine this analysis with registered audits of advantage rules and interventions on the availability of successful samples. The fixed-budget allocation experiment increases binary success signals on pilot-zero problems but fails the registered frontier-improvement rules and the per-problem model's forecast (\cref{sec:allocation}). Answer-budget checks qualify the supervised warm-start gain as largely a budget effect and reveal an interim reversal of a human-solution arm's position relative to Dr.\ GRPO (\cref{sec:budget}). Pre-registration~18 tests the model's response to a deliberate change in rollout allocation. In the registered point forecast comparison, both model variants confirm the HR and AV forecasts, but neither confirms AC: its observed mean matched-accuracy gain is $-0.05$ points, against $+4.12$ from the frozen law and $+2.46$ from deeper initial counts. The allocation diagnostics establish that AC supplies additional successful samples to pilot-zero groups, so the failed forecast accompanies a realized change in the sampling mechanism. Deeper initialization counts reduce the forecast discrepancy without resolving it. The model's agreement with aggregate pass@16 in the earlier audits therefore supports a conditional descriptive fit, while its use to guide this allocation intervention is unsupported. The warm-start failure and sensitivity to initialization further limit a mechanistic interpretation. The expected-update identities constrain gradient estimators; they do not validate the assumed dynamics of learning and transfer across problems.
